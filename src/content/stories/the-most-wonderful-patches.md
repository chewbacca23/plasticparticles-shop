Sitting in front of me are two small patches from Café du Cycliste in Nice, each one a tiny piece of the Alpes Maritimes stitched into fabric. They are the kind of souvenir that means more to a cyclist than any postcard could, because they carry the weight of climbs rather than just the memory of a shop visit. One says Col de la Madeleine. The other says Col d'Èze. Together they tell two very different stories about what it means to ride in this part of France.

The Col de la Madeleine patch is the bigger story in terms of scale of what you have to do as a rider yourself. This is one of the giants of the French Alps, a climb that regularly appears in the Tour de France and has broken plenty of legs over the decades. Depending on which side you approach from, you are looking at somewhere close to twenty kilometers of climbing, gaining well over fifteen hundred meters in altitude. It is long, it is unforgiving in the middle sections, and it rewards patience more than raw power. Riders who have done it often describe the sensation of watching the valley shrink beneath them for what feels like an eternity, switchback after switchback, tree line giving way to open pasture and then to bare rock near the summit. Wearing or displaying a patch for a climb like this is almost a badge of suffering, a nod to the kind of day where your legs remember every pedal stroke long after you have showered and eaten dinner. It represents effort measured in hours, not minutes.

The Col d'Èze patch, meanwhile, tells a shorter but no less iconic tale. This climb rises above Nice itself, connecting the coast to the medieval village of Èze perched high on its rocky outcrop overlooking the Mediterranean. It is nowhere near as long or as brutal as the Madeleine, but it holds its own kind of legendary status among cyclists, largely because of its history with Paris Nice, the race often nicknamed the Race to the Sun. For years the Col d'Èze served as the finishing climb of that race, a short sharp test that decided overall winners in the closing kilometers of a week long battle across France. There is something poetic about a climb so tied to the idea of arrival, since it quite literally brings riders from the interior of the country down to the sea, then straight back up into the hills above it. Riding it yourself, even without a race number pinned to your jersey, gives you a small taste of that drama, especially with the turquoise water visible behind you as you climb.

What makes these two patches such a nice pair is the contrast they represent. The Madeleine speaks to endurance, to the high mountains, to the kind of climb you plan a whole day around and maybe even a whole trip. The Col d'Èze speaks to something more immediate and local, a climb you could knock out before lunch if you are staying anywhere near Nice, yet one loaded with racing pedigree. Between the two, you get a sense of the range that cycling in this region offers, from the wild high Alps to the sunbaked hills right behind the Riviera.

There is also something satisfying about buying these patches specifically in Nice, at Café du Cycliste. The brand itself grew out of this coastal cycling culture, founded by people who wanted to bring a bit of French elegance and functionality together in cycling clothing, and their shop in Nice acts almost like a shrine to the local riding scene. Picking up a patch there feels different than ordering one online from somewhere far away. You are buying it in the shadow of the very hills it depicts, maybe even after having ridden one of them that same morning, still slightly sore and smelling of sunscreen and effort.

Patches like these end up on jersey sleeves, on musette bags, on the back pockets of a favorite cycling cap, or sometimes simply pinned to a corkboard next to route maps and old race numbers. They are quiet markers of personal history, a way of saying I was there without needing to explain the whole story every time someone asks. Anyone who has ridden either of these climbs will understand exactly what the patch means the moment they see it. Anyone who has not will hopefully ask about it, giving you the perfect excuse to talk about switchbacks, sea views, or thighs burning somewhere above the tree line.

In the end these two small pieces of embroidered fabric hold an entire regional cycling identity in miniature. The high alpine drama of the Madeleine and the coastal charm of the Col d'Èze sit side by side, a reminder that this part of France offers both extremes within a short drive of each other. Whether they end up sewn onto something you wear regularly or kept as small keepsakes in a drawer, they are proof of two very different days on the bike, both worth remembering.

And the best thing is I got them as a present form the guys that handed me coffee as well. It was pure bliss.
