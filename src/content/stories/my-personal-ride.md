  I don't remember the exact day I first saw the frame. I remember the workshop, though: the smell of resin, the hum of a small fan, and Andreas bent over a bench with the patience of someone who had never once been in a hurry. This was back when his shop was still in Berlin. Bikes were being born in that room one layer at a time, and I was lucky enough to be standing in it.


  He is a friend, and that changes the whole story. I didn't order a product. I sat in a room with a person who wanted to understand how I ride, where I ride, and what I want from a bike when the road tilts upward and my legs start negotiating. He listened more than he talked. Then he went away and made something that, as far as I can tell, exists nowhere else in the world.


  The frame carries the name Montecruz. Carbon, handmade, and light in a way that has little to do with the number on a scale. It feels alive when you lift it. You can sense that someone decided each curve on purpose.


  Not long after, Andreas packed up the Berlin workshop and moved to Switzerland. I understood it, because if you build bikes with that much care, the Alps are probably where you want to be. But it changed what the bike means to me. It stopped being just a frame and became a marker of a time and a place, a piece of Berlin cycling that left the city and took a little of it along. When I ride it through Berlin now, it feels as if the bike remembers where it was born.


  Building it up


  A frame like that deserves a build with opinions, and I have opinions.


  The Shimano Dura-Ace groupset went on first, because I wanted shifting that disappears. You think about the next gear and it's already there, with that crisp mechanical confidence Shimano does better than anyone. It never asks for attention. It just works, ride after ride, and that kind of reliability turns into trust.


  The wheels were a longer conversation with myself. In the end I went with ENVE rims laced to DT Swiss 240 hubs, and I have never regretted it. The 240s are almost embarrassingly good: smooth, quiet, and so well made that they're the kind of part you appreciate most when you're not thinking about them. On a long descent, with the freehub ticking down to silence, you can feel how little friction there is between you and the road.


  Then the René Herse tires, which taught me something I thought I already knew. I used to believe speed lived in narrow, hard tires and stiff everything. René Herse turned that upside down for me. Supple casings, a bit more width, and suddenly the road isn't something you fight. The rough stretches of Berlin asphalt, the patched-up bike paths, the gravel I wasn't supposed to take: all of it gets absorbed, and I arrive fresher and somehow faster. The bike feels like it's floating a few millimetres above the surface.


  The cockpit is a 3T handlebar, where my hands have spent more hours than I could count and never once complained. Under me is the Brooks carbon saddle, which I think is a perfect little joke about the whole bike: an old English name with a long leather-and-rivets reputation, rebuilt in modern carbon. Old craft, new material. My feet lock into Look pedals, and that click is the sound of the ride starting for real.


  And on the front sits the Wahoo, quietly logging everything: distance, speed, elevation, heart rate. I glance at it when I'm curious. On my best days I forget it's there.


  Ventoux


  Every bike gets one ride that defines it. For mine it was Mont Ventoux.


  I climbed it from Malaucène with Bruno and Henri. If you know the mountain, you know the feeling at the bottom, that mix of excitement and low-level dread. It's a big, bald, unforgiving thing sitting on the horizon, and you've told yourself you're doing this for fun.


  The first part is deceptive. Trees, shade, a steady rhythm. Then the forest gives way and the mountain shows you what it really is: white stone, wind, and a road that just keeps going up. There's a stretch where the whole world narrows to the wheel in front of you, your breathing, and the next pedal stroke. Bruno and Henri were there with me, a few metres ahead, a few metres behind, the three of us swapping the kind of silence that only happens when everyone is suffering equally and nobody minds.


  The bike never let me down. It climbed like it wanted to. The frame, the wheels, the light build: it all added up to something that felt less like riding a machine and more like moving together with something that understood the assignment. I won't pretend the legs weren't doing the work, but the bike made sure none of my effort went to waste.


  And then the summit, the weather-beaten observatory tower, the wind, the little crowd of exhausted people all wearing the same grin.


  Then the descent.


  I set a personal record on the way down: 118 km/h. I'll say the number once and let it sit, because it still doesn't feel real. In that moment the bike was completely calm. No shake, no nervousness, just a long, stable, precise line down the mountain, the wheels humming, the tires gripping, the frame doing exactly what Andreas built it to do. I remember thinking that I was going this fast and had never felt safer. That's not luck. That's a person who knew what he was doing, years earlier, in a workshop in Berlin.


  At the bottom I was laughing, and my hands were still shaking a little, and I turned around to look back up at where we'd come from.


  What it is to me


  People sometimes ask why anyone would spend this much time and care on a bike. The honest answer is that it stopped being about the parts a long time ago.


  Every component on it is something I chose: some quickly, some after months of reading and doubting. But the heart of it is the frame, and the frame is a person. It's Andreas, and the fact that a friend made this with his own hands. Every time I clip in, I'm riding a bit of his patience and his skill and his refusal to rush.


  He lives in Switzerland now, far from the workshop where this began. Some day I'd love to ride there with him, on his roads, in his mountains, on the bike he made. I'd want him to see how it turned out, how many miles it has, and how much it has given me.


  Until then, it waits by the door. Every morning it's there, quiet and ready, and every time I roll out I'm reminded that some things in life are worth doing slowly, worth doing by hand, and worth doing for someone you care about.


  That's what Montecruz is.
