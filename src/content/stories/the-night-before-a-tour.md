# Chasing the Peloton to the Sea: The Weekend at the Tour de France Femmes Finale in Nice

There are trips you plan for months, spreadsheet and all, and there are trips that start as a throwaway line in a group chat, "wait, the women's Tour finishes in Nice this year?", and turn into a plane ticket before you've even finished your coffee. Mine was the second kind. One minute I was scrolling through the route announcement for the 2026 Tour de France Femmes avec Zwift, half paying attention, and the next I had a hotel booked three streets back from the Promenade des Anglais and a very smug grin on my face. Some decisions just make themselves.

I'd followed the men's Tour for years the way most cycling fans do, half religiously, half by osmosis, checking results over lunch, half-watching the mountain stages with one eye while pretending to work. But the women's race had been climbing up my list of favorites for a couple of seasons now, and 2026 felt like the year to stop watching it through a screen and actually go stand on a mountainside and scream at strangers on bicycles. Turns out that's a genuinely great way to spend a weekend.

## Getting There: The Long Way Round to the French Riviera

I'm not going to pretend the logistics were glamorous. Nice in early August is not exactly an undiscovered secret, it's the French Riviera at the absolute peak of summer, stacked with beachgoers, yacht owners, and now, on top of all that, several hundred thousand cycling fans converging on one stretch of coastline for the finale of a Grand Tour. Flights were pricier than I wanted to admit out loud, and every hotel search turned into a small archaeology project, digging past sold-out listings to find something that wasn't a forty-minute tram ride from anything.

But I've learned that the annoying parts of a trip evaporate almost instantly once you're actually there, and they did. I landed at Nice Côte d'Azur airport in the early afternoon, stepped outside, and got hit with that particular wall of Mediterranean heat and salt air that immediately tells you you've left home behind. The airport practically sits on the water, you can see the bay from the runway, and the tram into the city hugs the coastline just enough that by the time I'd checked into my hotel and dumped my bag, I'd already caught glimpses of the sea about six different times. Not a bad way to arrive anywhere.

The whole city already had a slightly different texture than a normal August in Nice. Barriers were going up along sections of road that clearly weren't there the week before. Fan zones were half-assembled in public squares, all scaffolding and half-hung banners with the race's logo. Delivery vans marked with team names idled outside hotels while soigneurs unloaded bike boxes and crates of bottles. You could feel the city bracing for something, in the good way, the way a town feels the night before a festival, buzzing under the surface even though most of the actual noise hasn't started yet.

I wandered down toward the Promenade des Anglais that first evening mostly to get my bearings, and found the finish line structure already half-built: the inflatable arch, the barriers being unrolled in long metal ribbons, a stage set up for podium presentations facing the sea. It's a strange, quiet thrill to see the actual physical apparatus of a race you've only ever watched on television getting assembled in front of you, piece by piece, like watching a film set go up before the actors arrive. I stood there for probably twenty minutes just looking at an empty finish line, absurdly excited about a stretch of tarmac.

## Saturday: Sisteron to Nice, and the Long Wait on the Roadside

The race itself had been unfolding for a week already by the time I got there, riders had started in Lausanne, crossed into France, tackled a mountain stage that took them over Mont Ventoux, fought out a bunch sprint finish in one town and a lone breakaway win in another. I'd been following the results from afar the way you do with a race you haven't caught up with in person yet, and by the time Stage 8 rolled around, the transition from Sisteron into Nice, the longest stage of the race at 175 kilometers, the general classification battle had tightened into exactly the kind of contest you dream about seeing live: a rider in yellow trying to hold on, and a rival circling, waiting for the right mountain to attack.

I decided to watch Stage 8 from a spot in the hills just outside the city, where the route started to bite before the long run down toward the coast. Getting there meant an early start, a regional train, and then a fairly serious uphill walk with a small crowd of other fans who all had the same idea, all of us sweating through our shirts before ten in the morning and not caring even slightly. There's a specific kind of camaraderie that forms among people standing on a mountainside for hours waiting for a bike race to arrive, you end up chatting with whoever's next to you, sharing sunscreen, trading guesses about time gaps based on nothing but vibes and the occasional radio update crackling from someone's portable speaker.

The wait is, I think, underrated as part of the experience. You get there hours early because you have to, because once the road closes that's it, no more shuffling around for a better view. So you sit in the shade of whatever tree claims the least amount of direct August sun, you eat the sandwich you optimistically packed at 6 a.m. and you watch the caravan go by, the publicity convoy that precedes the race by a good hour, blaring music, throwing out branded hats and keychains and little bags of sweets to anyone who reaches out a hand. I caught a cap from a coffee sponsor and a small bag of something suspiciously sugary from a snack brand, and felt, for a moment, exactly nine years old.

Then there's the second wait, after the caravan, before the race, which is quieter, more expectant. Someone's radio picks up race coverage in French, and even with my patchy grasp of the language I could follow the rhythm of it: names repeated, time gaps read out, that rising, tightening cadence commentators get when a gap is closing or a rider is cracking. A murmur runs through the roadside crowd whenever a name gets mentioned that everyone recognizes. And then, from somewhere down the valley, you hear it before you see it, the helicopters first, thin and distant, then the motorbikes, then that specific hum of tires and gears that a peloton makes, which is somehow both quieter and more urgent in person than it ever sounds on a broadcast.

They came through in a blur. That's the thing nobody quite prepares you for, after hours of anticipation, the actual passing of the race takes maybe fifteen seconds for the front group, and it is completely, thrillingly overwhelming. A wall of color and noise and speed, close enough to feel the air move, gone almost before your brain catches up to what it just watched. I'd read plenty of race reports over the years describing the "shock of how fast they're actually going," and I'd nodded along, sure, fast, got it, but nothing about reading that sentence prepared me for the reality of professional cyclists coming past at a climbing pace that still outstrips anything I could manage on a flat road with a tailwind. I may have made a genuinely undignified noise.

The front of the race came through together that day, a compact, focused group rather than a lone flyer, which told its own story even before I checked the results properly later that evening back at the hotel, the stage had rewarded a solo rider who'd gone clear earlier on, but behind her, the GC contenders had spent the day watching each other rather than attacking outright, each waiting for the terrain of the final stage to do the deciding. Reading the details afterward, sprawled on my hotel bed with sunburnt shoulders and aching legs from all that uphill walking, I felt like I'd watched a chess match at 45 kilometers an hour without fully realizing it in the moment. That's the strange trade-off of watching from the roadside instead of on a screen, you get the visceral, physical reality of the race, the speed and the sound and the sheer scale of the thing, but you lose the overview, the tactical narrative that a broadcast stitches together for you afterward. I didn't mind the trade at all. I'll take the fifteen seconds of chaos over the tidy story any day, and I got the tidy story anyway, later, over a very well-earned beer.

## The City the Night Before the Finale

Nice on the Saturday evening before the final stage had fully transformed. Restaurants had race coverage playing on screens normally reserved for football. Bars along the old town's narrow streets were packed with fans comparing notes on where to watch the next day, trading tips about which corner of the Col d'Èze circuit offered the best combination of shade, view, and phone signal. I found myself at a small table outside a place that smelled overwhelmingly of garlic and grilled fish, eating something with far too much butter in it, listening to a table of French fans a few feet away arguing passionately about tactics for the next day's stage in a way that required zero translation to understand the emotional stakes involved.

The final stage, Stage 9, a circuit race entirely within and around Nice, had a reputation before I'd even arrived. Everyone I spoke to, French fans and fellow travelers alike, talked about the Col d'Èze the way people talk about a well-known villain: with a kind of gleeful dread. The route sends the peloton up the climb four times over the course of ninety-nine kilometers, three ascents via the gentler, longer eastern approach, and then, on the final and most decisive lap, a shorter but considerably steeper route via the Chemin des Vinaigriers, with one particularly brutal middle kilometer that ramps up well past anything reasonable. After that final summit, it's a long technical descent back down toward the coast and the finish line on the Promenade des Anglais. Short and vicious, everyone agreed, which after a Saturday of watching the race unfold at 175 kilometers of attrition sounded almost like a relief. It wasn't.

I didn't sleep especially well that night, which I choose to attribute to excitement rather than the amount of coffee I'd had trying to stay alert through the previous day's heat.

## Sunday: Four Trips Up the Col d'Èze

Race day for the finale had a completely different energy from Saturday. Because the route was a repeating circuit rather than a point-to-point stage, spectators had the rare luxury of choosing a single great vantage point and getting to see the race pass by not once, but multiple times, a genuinely brilliant piece of course design if you're a fan rather than a broadcaster trying to fill hours of coverage. I picked a spot roughly two-thirds of the way up the climb, on the steep, punchy section that photographs so well in every preview I'd read in the lead-up: pine trees, a view down over the rooftops toward the sea, and, crucially, actual shade, which by midday in Nice in August is not a detail to take lightly.

The crowd built steadily through the morning. Families with folding chairs and cooler bags. A cluster of fans in full team kit, presumably diehard supporters of one rider or another, who'd clearly done this before and had brought cowbells, actual cowbells, the kind you'd associate with an Alpine men's stage rather than a Riviera circuit race. Someone nearby had a small portable radio tuned to French race commentary, and a rotating cast of strangers gathered around it throughout the day to get updates, myself very much included, nodding along and picking out names and numbers even when the rest of the sentence went past too fast to catch.

Watching the same climb four times over an afternoon does something interesting to your sense of the race. The first pass, early in the stage, was almost sedate by comparison to what I knew was coming, the peloton still largely together, riders chatting, saving themselves, the tension present but banked low, like a fire that hasn't caught yet. By the second pass, gaps had started opening at the back, riders who'd blown early falling away from the group, and you could feel the crowd's attention sharpen slightly, everyone doing quiet mental math about who was where. By the third ascent, the group at the front had thinned to something recognizable, the GC contenders, the climbers, the ones everyone had actually come to see, and the roadside noise ratcheted up correspondingly, every name shouted a little louder, every gap between groups met with an audible reaction from the crowd.

Then came the fourth and final climb, up the steeper side, and this was the one everybody had been waiting for. Word filtered through the crowd via the radio and via shouts relayed up the hillside from spectators further down, a small group had gone clear on the previous descent, and within it, exactly the two riders the whole race had quietly been building toward: the rider in yellow, and her closest challenger, both of them isolated from teammates, both of them fully aware that whatever happened on this final ascent would likely decide the entire general classification.

I will be honest: I did not watch this with anything resembling composure. When they came into view, noticeably slower than the earlier passes, visibly working, the challenger clearly pressing the pace and the yellow jersey holder matching her, refusing to let a gap open, the entire hillside around me erupted. Cowbells, whistles, people running alongside the barriers for a few meters shouting encouragement in three or four languages at once. I found myself shouting too, for riders whose names I'd only ever previously said out loud to a television screen, and it did not feel remotely embarrassing in the moment, though writing it down now I recognize it probably looked exactly as unhinged as it felt.

They came past locked together, matching each other pedal stroke for pedal stroke on a gradient that I would frankly struggle with on a good day with no other humans around, and disappeared over the crest of the climb still shoulder to shoulder. From my spot on the hillside, that was the last I saw of them in person, from there, the race dropped down the technical descent toward the coast and I had a decision to make about how to spend the next forty-five minutes.

I chose to run. Not gracefully, not quickly by any competitive standard, but with real purpose, down through a side path a few other spectators had clearly used before me, weaving through backyards and a startled-looking group having lunch on their terrace, trying to beat both the crowds and the road closures down to somewhere near the finish. I did not entirely succeed, arriving at a spot a few hundred meters back from the actual finish line, packed in about four rows deep among fans who'd had the good sense to claim their spot hours earlier. But I could see a big screen from where I stood, and that turned out to be more than enough.

## The Finish on the Promenade des Anglais

What I watched, secondhand through a jumbotron with a crowd of strangers who reacted as one organism to every twist of the final kilometers, was this: on the descent from that final brutal ascent of the Col d'Èze, the pressure the challenger had applied on the climb hadn't been quite enough to break the race leader, instead, it was the leader who found something extra on the way down, opening a gap of around half a minute and taking that advantage alone through the technical final descent and out onto the flatter run back into the city. She held that gap all the way to the Promenade, soloing to the stage win and sealing the overall title in genuinely emphatic fashion, exactly the kind of dominant, no-doubt-about-it finish that turns a good final stage into an instant classic.

The roar around that big screen when she crossed the line was something else entirely, not quite the same as being trackside for the finish itself, I'm sure, but a wholly different, communal kind of electricity, several hundred strangers who'd spent their whole day and in some cases their whole week building toward this exact moment, all letting it out at once. I high-fived someone I will never see again in my life. A woman next to me was openly crying, the good kind of crying, and I understood completely.

Behind the overall drama, the sprint for the stage placings played out its own smaller stories on that same finishing straight, the kind of details you only really appreciate afterward, scrolling through results with a cold drink in hand: fast finishers who'd survived the brutal climbing all week finally getting their moment on a flat run to the line, a reminder that a Grand Tour, even a shortened, ferocious nine-day one like this, tells dozens of stories at once, not just the headline one written in yellow.

I made my way down toward the Promenade itself once the crowds had thinned enough to move, just to stand where the finish line had been, now littered with confetti and the debris of a race just finished, discarded water bottles, torn barrier tape, the particular emptied-out feeling a place gets right after something enormous has happened in it. The sea was doing its early-evening thing, turning that impossible shade of blue-gold that the Riviera does so well, and somewhere behind me a podium ceremony was happening that I couldn't get close enough to actually watch, though I could hear the crowd noise rise and fall with each name that was announced.

## Coming Down From It

That evening felt like the exhale after holding your breath for two days straight. I ended up at a bar with two fans from Belgium I'd first spoken to on the hillside that morning, comparing photos and video clips, all of us still slightly too loud, still narrating the day back to each other even though we'd all just lived through the exact same thing forty minutes apart. There's something lovely about that kind of instant, temporary friendship that big shared events produce, for one night we were the closest thing to old friends, bonded entirely by having stood on the same hot mountainside shouting at the same two riders, and by the next morning I never saw either of them again, and that felt exactly right.

I stayed one more day after the race ended, mostly to actually see Nice without a bike race running through the middle of it, the old town in daylight without barriers everywhere, a swim in a sea that had spent the whole weekend as a backdrop rather than a destination, a very long, very indulgent lunch with genuinely no schedule to keep. But the city still had the race's afterglow all over it: banners not yet taken down, the finish-line arch still standing forlornly on the Promenade like a stage set the morning after the show, café conversations still circling back to the stage even a full day later. It's strange how much a place can be defined for a weekend by an event that's already, technically, over.

## Why It Was Worth It

If you'd told me a few years ago that I'd fly to the south of France specifically to stand on the side of a mountain road for six hours in order to watch professional athletes ride past me at speed for a grand total of maybe two minutes, I'd have believed you, honestly, that's more or less the deal with watching any bike race live, and anyone who's done it knows the arithmetic doesn't sound great on paper. But the arithmetic misses the point entirely. It's not really about those two minutes, or even four, if you're lucky enough to catch a circuit stage with repeat passes. It's the whole day built around them, the walk up in the morning heat, the caravan and its ridiculous freebies, the radio commentary passed hand to hand through a crowd of strangers, the particular tension of hearing a gap close before you can see it, the noise a hillside makes when the race everyone's been waiting for all week finally comes past for real.

And it's the city itself, transformed for a weekend into something a little larger than its normal self, barriers and fan zones and a finish line built out of scaffolding on a beachfront promenade that spends the other 363 days of the year just being a beachfront promenade. Nice was beautiful before the Tour de France Femmes arrived and it'll be beautiful again long after everyone's gone home, but for those two days in August it was something more specific and more electric than just beautiful, and I got to stand in the middle of it.

I'm already trying to work out where the race finishes next year.
