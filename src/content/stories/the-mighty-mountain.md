# The Giant of Provence, Conquered My Way

There are climbs you do because they are on the way. Then there are climbs you do because they have been sitting in the back of your mind for years. Mont Ventoux is the second kind. Everyone who rides a bike seriously knows its name before they ever see it. Everyone who has seen it knows why it carries the nickname "The Giant of Provence." It rises out of the Provençal countryside like something that does not belong there. A lonely limestone dome towers over lavender fields and vineyards. It is visible from towns thirty or forty kilometers away on a clear day. On the day I finally rode it, the sky was about as clear as it gets.

I chose to climb from Malaucène. Locals and seasoned Ventoux riders will tell you this is the harder of the classic ascents. It is tougher than the famous route from Bédoin that most cyclists default to. Malaucène does not offer the same rhythm. It throws ramps at you early. It backs off just enough to make you think you have found your legs. Then it reintroduces the pain in the forest section with a relentlessness that never really lets up. That continues until you break out above the treeline. That is the moment everyone talks about. The trees stop and the landscape turns into that otherworldly, lunar expanse of white scree. It makes Ventoux look like the surface of the moon rather than a mountain in the south of France. The wind picks up there too. It funnels across the exposed slope with nothing to slow it down. You start to understand why this mountain has broken so many riders, professionals included.

I was not racing anyone officially that day. But if you ride with people, you know there is always a race happening whether you admit it or not. This time I came up about half an hour after the boys. That is not my finest split on paper. There was a moment somewhere past Le Chalet Reynard when I wondered if I had gone out too conservatively at the base. My legs were burning and my lungs were working overtime in that thin, dry mountain air. But climbing Ventoux was never really about beating anyone else up that mountain. It is about getting up it at all. It is about finishing what you told yourself you would do. So I kept turning the pedals. I found a rhythm somewhere past the Tom Simpson memorial, that solemn stone marker that always makes you sit up a little straighter out of respect. Eventually the summit tower came into view. The old weather station stood at the top like a lighthouse for cyclists.

Reaching the summit of Mont Ventoux is a strange kind of relief. Your body wants to collapse. The view refuses to let you look away. From up there you can see across almost the entire region. The patchwork of Provence spreads out below in every direction. On the clearest days you can even see the Alps in the distance. The wind, always the wind, cuts straight through whatever jacket you thought would be enough. I stayed up there for about half an hour. Partly to recover. Partly because you do not rush a summit like that. I ate, drank, and took in the view. I watched other riders arrive in ones and twos with the same mixture of exhaustion and disbelief on their faces that I probably had myself. Half an hour at altitude, wind battering the summit building, felt like I had earned every minute of stillness after the effort it took to get there.

Then came the part I had actually been looking forward to since I first saw the profile of this mountain on a map. The descent. Whatever ground I lost on the way up, I was determined to make up for on the way down. Ventoux puts so much suffering into your legs on the ascent. But it gives you something extraordinary in return once you point the bike downhill. The road from the summit is wide and smooth. It sweeps in long, fast curves that seem designed for exactly this kind of speed. I tucked in. I trusted the bike. I trusted the tires. I trusted the road. And I let it run. Somewhere on one of those long straights, tucked as low as I could manage with the wind screaming past my helmet, I glanced down and saw 118 km/h on the computer. That is a new personal record, and one I do not think I will forget anytime soon. There is a particular kind of quiet that happens at that speed. It is not literal silence, since the wind is roaring in your ears. It is a mental quiet, where nothing exists except the road unspooling in front of you. Everything narrows to the absolute focus required to hold your line. It is terrifying and exhilarating in equal measure. That is exactly the reason so many of us keep coming back to mountains like this one.

By the time I rolled into town at the bottom, my legs were still slightly shaking. That was as much from the adrenaline as from the effort. I found a café and ordered a coffee. It was the kind of coffee that tastes better after a day like that than it has any right to. I sat outside and watched the mountain from a distance for a change, instead of climbing straight up its flank. I waited for Bruno and Henri to come down. It took about twenty minutes before they rolled in. We sat there together, coffee cups in hand, going over the day. We talked about who felt good and who suffered where. We talked about the wind at the top and the exact spot on the descent where each of us had hit our fastest speed. We compared notes the way cyclists always do after a big ride, half bragging and half debriefing, entirely happy.

Looking back on it now, the half hour deficit on the way up does not sting the way it might have in the moment. What stays with me is the whole shape of the day. The brutal, honest climb from Malaucène. The half hour at the summit with that view stretching out in every direction. The sheer speed of the descent and that 118 km/h flashing on the screen. And then the simple, quiet satisfaction of a coffee in the sun with friends, waiting for them to arrive, trading stories about a mountain that had just given all three of us exactly what we came for. Wonderful it was. It is the kind of day that makes you start planning the next one before you have even finished your coffee.
