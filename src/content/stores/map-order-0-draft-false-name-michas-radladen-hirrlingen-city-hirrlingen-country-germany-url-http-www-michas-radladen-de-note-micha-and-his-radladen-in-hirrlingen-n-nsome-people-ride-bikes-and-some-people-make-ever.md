  Micha and His Radladen in Hirrlingen


  Some people ride bikes, and some people make everyone around them better for being on one. Micha is both. If you ever find yourself in Hirrlingen, a small place in the south of Germany, and you see a bike shop with a name that says exactly what it is, Michas Radladen Hirrlingen, you have found one of the warmest corners of the German cycling world. And you will understand quickly that the man behind the counter is the reason.


  From Berlin's boards to the Swabian hills


  Micha came out of Berlin, and anyone who knows the city's cycling scene knows it has a particular character: a bit rough around the edges, honest, unpretentious, and fast when it counts. Micha was a track rider there. Track racing is a discipline with nowhere to hide. There are no gears to shift your way out of trouble, no descents to recover on, and no team car waiting behind you. It is just you, a fixed gear, a steep banked oval, and the people around you. It teaches you rhythm, humility and precision, and it teaches you to trust your equipment completely, which is a good school for someone who would later spend his life looking after other people's bikes.


  What stayed with him from those years is that he never became a show-off. Micha is just the plain rider he always was. He doesn't need the newest kit or a dramatic story, and he doesn't talk about his past results to impress anyone. He gets on the bike, he rides, he enjoys it, and he is happy when you enjoy it too. In a sport that can get obsessed with watts, brands and Strava segments, that attitude is a gift.


  Where we met: Bozen to Venice


  I met Micha in Italy, on the Adidas tour that took us from the north, near Bozen, all the way down to Venice. If you have never done a ride like that, picture it: mountain air at the start, long days in the saddle, the landscape slowly flattening and softening as you roll toward the lagoon, the espresso stops, the tired laughter at the end of each stage. A tour like that is a small travelling world, and in every travelling world there is someone who holds it together.


  For us, that was Micha. He was the support guy, and I want to be clear about what that meant. It was not a job where you hand out water bottles and wave from a van. He took care of our bikes, all of them. He looked after the complete team, and he did it with a calm and generosity that I can still picture. Whenever something rattled, slipped, rubbed or clicked, he was already there. Often he had noticed it before the rider had. He worked late, he started early, and he never made anyone feel like a burden for needing help. Anyone who has ever stood at the roadside with a broken derailleur knows how rare that is.


  The thing is, I can't tell you how much we bonded on that trip. It wasn't one big moment. It was a hundred small ones: a quick fix before the start, a joke at dinner, a few quiet words when the climb was harder than expected, and the shared feeling at the end of a long day that we were all in it together. By the time we reached Venice, he was no longer just the support guy. He was a friend, a wonderful mate, and someone I knew I would keep in my life.


  Why it matters that he looks after your bike


  There is something quietly beautiful about a good mechanic. When you hand your bike to someone, you are handing over the thing that carries you downhill at fifty kilometres an hour, that you trust with your bones and your skin. A mechanic who is careless, rushed or arrogant is a danger. A mechanic who is patient, thorough and caring is practically a guardian angel.


  Micha is the second kind. He treats every bike as if it belonged to someone he cares about, because to him it usually does. He explains things without talking down to you, tells you honestly what needs doing and what doesn't, and never tries to sell you something you don't need. That honesty is built into who he is, and you can feel it from the first conversation.


  Michas Radladen Hirrlingen


  Now Micha has his own store, and it is a wonderful one. After years of looking after other people's bikes and teams, he has built a place of his own where the same values he showed in Italy now greet every customer who walks through the door. It is a proper Radladen, a bike shop in the traditional sense: a place to talk about riding, to get a real repair, to be advised by someone who rides, and to leave feeling a bit better than when you arrived.


  In a time when so much cycling is bought online, in boxes, with no one to ask and no one to fix it when it goes wrong, a local shop like his matters enormously. It keeps a community alive. It gives the commuter, the weekend rider, the kid with a first proper bike and the old hand with a fifty-year-old steel frame the same warm welcome. A village like Hirrlingen is lucky to have it.


  I'm sure that people who come into the shop for a flat tyre end up staying for a chat, and that people who came for a chat leave with their brakes adjusted for free. That is how it goes with Micha. He is not running a shop just to sell things. He is running it because he loves bikes and loves the people on them.


  The rider underneath it all


  What I admire most is that none of this success has changed him. In Berlin he was a track rider, then the support guy in Italy, and now a shop owner in the south. Through all of it he has stayed the same: no fuss, no ego, just a genuine, plain rider with a big heart. Titles come and go, shops open and grow, but character stays, and his is the kind you want in your corner.


  If you ride, and you are ever in the south of Germany, do yourself a favour and visit him. Bring your bike, bring your stories, and bring a bit of time, because you will want to stay for a while.
