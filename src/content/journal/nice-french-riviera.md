![Dusk over the Baie des Anges, Nice](/stories/nice-baie-des-anges.jpg)

Ask a Parisian to name France's greatest city and you'll get Paris, obviously — loyalty runs deep in the capital. But ask anyone who has actually stood on the Promenade des Anglais at sunset, watched the Baie des Anges turn gold, and felt the Mediterranean breeze roll in off the water, and you'll get a different answer entirely. Nice isn't just another French city with nice weather and a pretty coastline. It is, in almost every way that matters, the most complete city France has to offer — a place where geography, history, culture, and daily life align in a way few other cities manage.

## A setting no other city can claim

Start with the obvious: the location. Nice sits where the Alps meet the sea, and that alone gives it a kind of geographic luck that Lyon, Bordeaux, or even Marseille can't quite match. Within an hour of the city center you can be skiing in the Mercantour mountains or swimming in the Mediterranean — the same day, if you're ambitious about it. The city itself curves around the Baie des Anges in a long, graceful arc, backed by hills dotted with terracotta rooftops and cypress trees. Paris has the Seine; Nice has an entire sea.

And it isn't just scenery for scenery's sake. The Promenade des Anglais, stretching along the waterfront for kilometers, is one of the great urban walks in Europe — built in the 19th century for wealthy English visitors escaping the winter, and still, two centuries later, the beating heart of the city. Cyclists, joggers, rollerbladers, and strolling families share it with a mix of grand belle-époque hotels and unbroken sea views that Nice's rivals simply don't have the coastline to offer.

## Old town charm without the crowds of Paris

Vieux Nice, the old town, is a labyrinth of narrow ochre-and-yellow streets, Baroque churches, and market stalls that feels genuinely lived-in rather than preserved for tourists. The Cours Saleya market fills every morning with flowers, socca stands, and the specific, unmistakable smell of Niçoise cooking — herbs, olive oil, and something always simmering nearby. It has all the charm people travel to Paris hoping to find in Montmartre, minus the crowds and the pickpockets.

This is a city that has managed something genuinely rare: it kept its Italian-influenced architecture and its distinct regional identity intact. Nice was part of the Kingdom of Sardinia until 1860, and that history is still visible everywhere — in the pastel palazzo facades, in the language (Niçard, a dialect closer to Ligurian than to standard French), and in a cuisine that owes as much to Genoa as it does to Provence. Few French cities can claim a cultural fingerprint this specific, this layered, or this delicious.

## A genuine capital of art

Nice's contribution to art history is wildly disproportionate to its size. Henri Matisse spent the last decades of his life here, drawn by what he called the special quality of the light along the coast, and the Musée Matisse now houses one of the most important collections of his work anywhere. Marc Chagall chose Nice too — the Musée National Marc Chagall was purpose-built to house his biblical-themed masterpieces, and it remains one of the most serene, thoughtfully curated museums in the country. Add in the MAMAC for contemporary art and a dense network of smaller galleries, and Nice quietly rivals Paris for pure artistic density per square kilometer, without ever needing to shout about it.

## The food alone is worth the trip

Niçoise cuisine deserves its own paragraph, because it's unlike anything else in France. Salade niçoise, obviously, but done right here rather than the tourist-menu version served everywhere else. Socca — a thin, blistered chickpea flatbread cooked in wood-fired ovens and eaten hot off the pan, ideally standing up on a street corner. Pissaladière, an onion tart with anchovies and olives that has more in common with a Ligurian focaccia than anything from northern France. Pan bagnat. Ratatouille, actually invented here, not in a Pixar kitchen. This is food shaped by the sea and the Italian border in equal measure, and it tastes like nowhere else in the country.

## Livable in a way few cities manage

Beyond the postcard version, Nice functions extraordinarily well as an actual place to live. The climate is mild nearly year-round — over 300 days of sunshine annually — which changes the entire rhythm of daily life. People eat outside. People walk. The tram system is clean and efficient, the airport sits practically in the city rather than an hour outside it, and the pace never tips into the frantic energy that can wear a person down in Paris. It has the cultural richness of a capital with the human scale of a much smaller town, and that combination is genuinely hard to find anywhere else in France.

## The verdict

Every French city has its partisans. Lyon has its gastronomy, Bordeaux its wine, Marseille its grit and its bouillabaisse, Paris its endless self-mythology. Nice doesn't need to compete on those terms, because it already has its own: mountains and sea in the same afternoon, an old town that feels authentically Mediterranean rather than curated, an art history that punches far above its size, a cuisine found nowhere else, and a climate that makes daily life feel less like a grind and more like something worth savoring.

Superb isn't too strong a word for it. Nice doesn't just belong among France's great cities — it makes a quiet, confident case for being the best of them.
